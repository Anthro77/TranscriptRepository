He discusses the Bronze Age Collapse. Around 1200 BC, the world was interconnected. The centers of civilization were Mycenaean Greece, Anatolia (modern Turkey), Canaan (modern Lebanon, Syria, Israel), Egypt, Mesopotamia (Iraq), Persia (Iran), Afghanistan, and India. Cyprus, Crete, Iberia, and Britain were also important.

It’s called the Bronze Age because bronze—an alloy of copper and tin—was the basis of the economy. Tin was found in Britain, Iberia, Anatolia, and Afghanistan; copper in Cyprus, Crete, and Anatolia. To make bronze, societies had to trade globally.

Ways to make money included mining, manufacturing, trading, and piracy. Strategic locations like Troy became extremely wealthy as trade hubs and toll points there. The Greeks attacked Troy, which was rich from trade, essentially as pirates.

Despite this interconnectedness, by 1200 BC, these civilizations collapsed. Mycenaean Greece was destroyed, the Hittite Empire fell, and Canaan ceased to be a global power. Egypt survived but lost dominance. The causes remain debated. Theories include invasions by the “Sea Peoples,” natural disasters like earthquakes, climate change, and internal revolts. The current scholarly view is a systems collapse caused by multiple factors over decades.

Peter Turchin’s theory of elite overproduction helps explain this. Societies with permanent hereditary elites gain organization, wealth, and scale but suffer from rent-seeking behavior—extracting resources without productive work. This creates debt, debt slavery, competition among elites, and internal instability. Societies eventually collapse when there are too many elites fighting for limited resources. Historical responses include land redistribution, civil war, or empire building—but all involve instability.

This pattern repeats throughout history. For example, the Mayan civilization collapsed similarly due to internal and structural pressures. Societies with egalitarian structures were more stable but vulnerable to conquest by organized, wealthy societies. Collapse often enables innovation: Greek civilization and Israelite society emerged after the Bronze Age Collapse, laying foundations for Western civilization and the Bible.

In summary: societies with too many elites who extract wealth without producing it are inherently unstable. Collapse is part of human history and can lead to progress. Understanding the cycle of elite overproduction helps explain why civilizations rise and fall.
