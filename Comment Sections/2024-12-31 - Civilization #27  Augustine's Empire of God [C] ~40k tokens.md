1. On moral innocence:
Augustine is right on the essential point: rape without consent does not stain the victim. Chastity (or any sexual virtue) is a matter of the will, not of what is done to a person. That’s a vital moral safeguard against victim-blaming.


2. On the suicide itself:
Suicide wasn’t morally required—and making it “exemplary” is dangerous. It converts an evil suffered into a new wrong against oneself and risks teaching survivors that death is preferable to living with undeserved shame. That’s a lesson we should never endorse.


3. On compassion and context:
Within Rome’s honor–shame code, Lucretia’s “choice” was heavily socially coerced. Her agency was constrained by expectations that equated reputation with virtue. Morally, that mitigates her blame: it was a tragic act under crushing norms. But mitigation is different from making it a model.


4. Across moral theories:



Deontological: suicide violates a duty to oneself; rape never imputes guilt to the victim.

Consequentialist: her death galvanized political change—but tying moral approval to political outcomes is perilous; it would justify harmful “examples.”

Virtue ethics: Roman honor saw courage in self-slaying; Christian/Aristotelian virtue would call the higher courage the willingness to live, speak truth, and seek justice despite slander.
