Kant’s central point in the Critique of Pure Reason is not that we can “control time and space” to control reality, nor that language creates the world. His claim is that the mind contributes the forms of experience (especially space and time) and uses concepts to organize sensory input, so we only ever know appearances as structured for us—not “things-in-themselves.” A more faithful Kantian bridge to Homer would be: shared concepts and narratives help coordinate experience socially, but they do not grant access to, or mastery over, noumenal reality.

Hegel’s Geist is also being stretched. Geist is not a cosmic memory-store or a supernatural “ghost internet.” It’s a name for historically developing mindedness—how meaning, institutions, and self-understanding unfold through social life over time. If we connect Hegel to Homer, the plausible claim is that epics can become part of a people’s Bildung: they shape the vocabulary through which a culture interprets conflict, honor, and community, and that vocabulary later shows up in institutions and public reasoning.

Jung’s collective unconscious, likewise, is not a literal archive of personal memories or a guarantee that one can “connect” to Homer’s consciousness. It is a hypothesis about inherited psychic structures (archetypes) that recur across cultures and can surface in myths, dreams, and art. On that reading, the Iliad’s durability is explained by its archetypal patterns—rage, honor, loss, reconciliation—rather than direct metaphysical access to dead individuals.

Shelley’s “poets are the unacknowledged legislators of the world” is best read as a thesis about imagination and moral perception: poetry enlarges sympathy, refines sensibility, and can precede changes in social norms and political possibility. Shelley is not making a technical epistemology claim like Kant, and he is not claiming poets literally manufacture reality; he’s arguing they reshape what societies can feel, value, and therefore eventually build.
