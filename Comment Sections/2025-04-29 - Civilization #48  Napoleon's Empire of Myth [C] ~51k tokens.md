Claude:
Lazare Carnot and the French Revolutionary Army laid important groundwork, but the corps system is most closely associated with Napoleon Bonaparte and his organizational genius. Napoleon formalized the corps structure during the early 1800s, making it a self-contained combined-arms formation typically containing infantry, cavalry, and artillery under a single commander (usually a marshal).

However, the conceptual origins trace back a bit earlier:

    Marshal de Broglie experimented with dividing the French army into semi-independent divisions during the Seven Years' War (1756–1763)
    Jacques Antoine Hippolyte, Comte de Guibert, a French military theorist, wrote influential works in the 1770s advocating for flexible, self-sufficient army divisions
    Jean-Baptiste de Gribeauval modernized French artillery, making combined-arms formations more practical

Napoleon refined these ideas into the corps d'armée system, where each corps (typically 20,000–30,000 soldiers) could operate independently, march on separate routes, and then concentrate for battle. This gave the Grande Armée tremendous strategic flexibility and was widely imitated by other European armies throughout the 19th century.
