### **@vincent_VAM** | 👍 8 likes
[View on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ft2CuowGuYc&lc=UgzIy_cZtSxprLwBqpt4AaABAg)

While reading, I keep circling back to the thought that if the characters just took a breath and thought to themselves: "If we stopped taking each other's wives and killing each other in revenge, wouldn't this all just end, and then we could go and live in peace?" That question feels so obvious to me that at first I almost felt frustrated reading the Iliad, like how can they not see this? I don't believe that no character makes this statement or proposes this as a peace option between the Greeks and Trojans.

But then I started realizing that maybe the problem isn't that they didn't see it. Maybe it's that the way I'm seeing it depends on ideas and concepts that didn't exist yet. Leading me down the rabbit hole of:

For this thought to exist in the time of Homer: "If we stopped taking each other's wives and killing each other in revenge, wouldn't this all just end, and we could live in peace?" For that to even make sense, I realized while thinking about it, that I'm already assuming a lot of things: 1) that all human beings in the time of Homer have equal moral value, even enemies; 2) that desire is something you're supposed to manage internally; 3)that violence can be part of a broken system rather than just "fate"; 4) that peace is "better" than honor.

I don't usually notice I'm assuming those things because they feel like the air I breathe. But what if none of that was available to them?

In the world of the Iliad 1) women aren't really treated as autonomous people in the way they are now. Marriage is closer to ownership than partnership. 2) Desire isn't something you're expected to reflect on... if you have the power to take something, taking it proves who you are. 3) Honor works like a zero-sum game: if someone takes from you and you don't retaliate, you loose status. 4) Violence isn't a breakdown of order; it is the mechanism that keeps order in place.

So when I think to myself, "Just don't do this and it will all stop" I'm realizing that wouldn't sound like a reasonable moral suggestion to them. It would sound more like "stop being what you are. Stop participating in reality as it exists."

It wouldn't be a small rule change for them, it would feel like the world falling apart.

I also notice that I'm reading the violence as a pattern that could be interrupted, like a loop that someone could step out of. But did they experience it that way? Or to them, revenge might not have been looked at as pathological; it was justice. Retaliation wasn't escalation; it was balance. Restraint wasn't virtue; it was weakness. Peace wasn't morally better; it was just what happened when no one had the strength to keep fighting.

And that's the part I'm still trying to wrap my head around... that there was no real outside position yet from which to say, "this whole system is wrong"... right? maybe?

That kind of thinking-stepping back, abstracting, seeing violence as a systemic problem instead of destiny, seems to require concepts that came much later. Things like systems thinking, individual moral restraint, equal moral worth between enemies and all human beings, an inner conscience that can govern desire, and the belief that peace is ethically better than honor.

Then I started to think "when did these ideas and concepts come into the world?" Maybe.... these ways of thinking didn't come all at once, that haven't always been here, but slowly began to emerge later, first in Greek Tragedy's moral ambiguity, in Plato's philosophy, in Stoicism, and eventually in Christianity's inversion of power and value i.e., "the weak inherit the earth."

I found it so fascinating that I'm bringing a whole moral framework with me that had to be invented over centuries. I always knew this in theory, abstractly you could say, but it wasn't always until reading the Iliad and following a train of thought, that this really hit home in a concrete way for the first time in my life. And maybe reading the Iliad feels unsettling because it shows a world before those ideas existed, which makes me wonder how stable they really are, and how easily a world could exist again where what feels obvious to me simply isn't there at all.

And then there is this other layer that I can't stop thinking about, which almost makes everything else click into place. The idea that in Homer there isn't really an interior "self" at all in the way I think of it now. That "most" of the thoughts that the characters have aren't even experienced as their own. When Achilles hesitates, it's Athena grabbing him. When Agamemnon makes a terrible decision, it's Zeus sending a dream. Even fear, courage, rage; they only partially form from inside the characters; mostly they arrive from outside. From gods. From forces. From the world. There's really no private inner space where someone weighs options, listens to their conscience, or says "maybe I shouldn't" or if there is, it seems primitive and quite underdeveloped. It makes me think the the "mind" 3000 years ago isn't really interior yet and they way I think of it now, it's distributed. It's always exterior. That the concept of an interior life/psychology literally had not been fully developed yet. Fascinating to me...

Which makes me think: "if there's no interiority, then where would moral restraint even live? Where would doubt live? Where would a thought like 'this cycle itself is wrong' come from, if thought itself isn't experienced as something YOU author from inside?" Meaning, intention, agency; all of it is externalized. It's public. Enacted. Witnessed by others that makes it real/substantial. So of course honor has to be public. Revenge public. Taking spoils public. Legacy creation public. Of course meaning has to be: "being remembered by others" because there's nowhere else to put it yet... or so it seems.

And that makes the Iliad feel less like a story about violent people and more like a story about a world before inwardness, before psychology, before conscience, before the idea that the "self" contains value. Which is wild to sit with, especially know that later cultures will "invent or develop" interiority, through tragedy, philosophy, Christianity, and then much later, Nietschze, Freud, etc. will come along and question just how stable that is.

That's why the Iliad feels so unsettling to me but also so alive to me. Because the Iliad isn't just showing a past that's different, it's showing a way of being human that is coherent without needing the very thing I live inside every day: my consciousness as self. And once I really see that, it's hard not to wonder how contingent all of this is. How much of what feels morally obvious to me now depends of fragile inventions that took centuries to form and how easily a world could exist again where those inventions simply aren't there at all. Which is honestly terrifying and fascinating all at once.

All of this sent me down one last personal rabbit hole: I asked myself why as a teenage did I want to be in the arts? Why did I want to make films specifically? Was it really about storytelling, or was it about not wanting to participate in the culture I found myself inside?--Like I felt myself saying I would not participate in the culture of Homer if i was born then-- Did I already feel, personally, as a teenager, that the dominant systems around me: money, power, ambitious, status- felt dirty or misaligned, even if I couldn't fully articulate why?

And yet, wasn't I still being shaped by those same systems? Because if I'm being honest I still wanted: recognition, legitimacy, etc-- a sense that I mattered because "status" is how meaning circulated int he world I grew up in and live in now?

Was art/film a way to hold both things at once? A way to avoid direct participation in what I rejected, my culture, while still hoping that success would allow me to receive status from the culture itself? Did I intuit that being an "artist" let me refuse the world symbolically, say: "I'm not like that," economically through work and direct participation--while remaining inside the "moral" or "status" economy that decides who counts? Was I trying to opt-out ethically while opting in socially?

When I think about reading Thoreau or other writers in high school, did I understand them only abstractly because I hadn't yet grasped how "deep" these value systems go? Did I think I could simply choose not to participate, without realizing that participation isn't just what you do for work, but the entire structure that produces meaning, recognition, and self-worth? Was I still living inside the ethics of my culture even as I tried to critique it?

And is that why reading the Iliad now feels so destabilizing? Because I'm finally seeing what it means to live inside a moral world without an outside; how people in Homer's time couldn't just step back and say this whole system is wrong because there was nowhere to stand to make that judgment? Am I realizing that I've been doing something similar; believing that I was outside my own culture, while still being shaped by its definitions of success and value?

If art was my third position; neither full participation nor total withdrawal, what happens when that position stops being enough? What happens when symbolic refusal no longer feels like refusal at all? Am I now asking a more dangerous question: not "how do I critique the world" but "what would it mean to actually live by a different set of values?" And am I only now, after reading the Iliad starting to see how much of my life was organized around 'managing' that tension, rather than 'resolving' it?

23:16 ​Here is a breakdown of where your memories "live":
​1. The Processing Center: The Hippocampus
​Think of the hippocampus as the brain's librarian. It doesn't store all the books, but it decides where they go.  
​Function: It is crucial for forming new memories and converting short-term memories into long-term ones.  
​Type: It handles declarative memories (facts, dates, and names) and episodic memories (specific life events).  
​2. Long-Term Storage: The Neocortex
​Once a memory is "consolidated" (usually over weeks or months, often while you sleep), it is transferred from the hippocampus to the neocortex.  
​Function: This is the outer layer of the brain where memories are stored permanently as general knowledge.  
​Note: This is why people with hippocampus damage can often remember their childhood (stored in the neocortex) but cannot remember what they had for breakfast today.
